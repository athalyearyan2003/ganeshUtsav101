# Pune Ganeshotsav — Project Context

> Persistent working context for designers, researchers, writers, prototypers, and AI assistants joining the Pune Ganeshotsav project. Read this before making product, UX, research, case-study, or presentation decisions.
>
> **Evidence discipline:** This file intentionally separates direct observations, interpretations, hypotheses, and design decisions. Never turn a hypothesis into a research finding or a UI decision into evidence that a problem exists.

---

## 0. Project at a glance

### One-line description

A service/ecosystem that helps people experience Pune Ganeshotsav by connecting **route planning, cultural discovery, contextual information, family/group support, and assistance** into one coherent festival companion.

### Central thesis

> **Pune Ganeshotsav is not simply a collection of destinations. It is a temporary, changing city experience in which navigation, cultural discovery, family coordination, and practical assistance happen simultaneously.**

### Current product direction

The project began as **My Family Ganeshotsav**, a family-centred route-planning concept. It has expanded into three connected layers:

1. **Journey / Route** — decide where to go and navigate around time, interests, group composition, mobility, rest, crowd tolerance, facilities, parking, and return needs.
2. **Discovery / Information** — understand Ganpatis, mandals, Dekhawas, stories, history, themes, and details worth noticing.
3. **Assistance / Support** — find contextual practical help such as rest, toilets, medical support, regrouping, safety, parking/return guidance, and relevant updates.

The **Home screen** is being developed as the central hub that connects these experiences.

### Do not frame this as

- a generic tourism app
- a Google Maps clone
- a festival encyclopedia
- a social feed
- a lifestyle/startup app
- an AI chatbot
- a giant feature dashboard
- a product invented first and justified afterward

### Better framing

A **civic / cultural festival companion** for a temporary urban environment, designed to help visitors make decisions, understand what they are experiencing, adapt to conditions, and coordinate around the people they came with.

# 1. Project origin and academic brief

The project originated from a system-thinking / Ganesh Festival design assignment. The supplied academic brief frames the festival as a complex living system involving visitors, mandals, businesses, police, infrastructure, waste management, traffic, public space, and other interacting components.

The assignment process emphasizes:

- plan and visit
- observe and document challenges
- reflect and shortlist
- define a clear problem statement
- generate product/service ideas
- connect ideas to a design specialization
- document photos, stories, and evidence

The assignment material explicitly encourages ground-level observation and warns against relying on unsupported assumptions.

### Important source note

Two supplied academic PDFs are dated 2025 and should be treated as **assignment/reference context**, not as proof that every 2026 festival condition remains the same.

# 2. Research question

The project has evolved from “choose one festival challenge” toward a broader system question:

> **How might we help people make sense of and move through Pune Ganeshotsav when the festival temporarily transforms the city into a dense, distributed, information-heavy environment?**

Supporting product formulation:

> **How might we connect planning, cultural discovery, and practical assistance so visitors can experience more of the festival with less uncertainty and friction?**

Family-centred formulation:

> **How might we plan the festival around the people coming with us, rather than only around the Ganpatis we want to visit?**

These are related formulations, not competing final statements.

# 3. Research method so far

The primary research is observational fieldwork. There are three distinct visits. **Do not merge Visit 2 and Visit 3.**

## Visit 1 — Murti selection / stalls around Balgandharva Chowk

### Direct observations

- Buyers inspected idols closely, especially eyes and details.
- What Ganpati was holding influenced selection.
- Style/type influenced decisions.
- Price was part of the decision.
- Family preferences mattered.
- People used phones and video calls to show idols to other family members and get opinions.
- Temporary stalls created crowding and limited space.
- Festival commerce occupied public space alongside normal civic rules.
- A **“NO HAWKERS”** sign was observed among the festival stalls.

### Interpretation / hypothesis

The decision to select a Ganpati can be socially distributed across people who are not physically at the stall.

### Design relevance

Potentially relevant to:

- family coordination
- shared decision-making
- digital + physical information exchange
- group-based festival planning

### Evidence boundary

Observed phone/video-call use supports the observation. It does **not** prove that most families select idols this way.

# 4. Visit 2 — Ganpati visit with grandmother during rain

This visit is separate from Visit 3.

### Direct observations

- Parking was relatively distant from darshan.
- Grandmother had difficulty accessing darshan.
- At one point she had to sit on the scooter because continuing through the environment was difficult.
- User had to keep track of her in the crowd.
- Later she experienced leg cramps.
- Heavy rain affected walking conditions.
- Umbrellas narrowed already-constrained paths.
- User’s umbrella broke.
- Bright LEDs / floodlights affected viewing, particularly with spectacles.
- Road litter/confetti was present.
- Scooter parking was dense.
- Barricades prevented vehicles from entering some spaces and enabled pedestrian movement.

### Stronger human framing

This is not simply an “elder accessibility” story. It is a combined experience of:

- distance
- weather
- crowding
- rest
- viewing conditions
- group coordination
- family responsibility

### Interpretation / hypothesis

A single family member’s mobility or stamina can change the whole outing, not just one accessibility detail.

### Important wording rule

Do not generalize to “grandparents cannot access Ganpatis.” A more defensible statement is:

> **The combination of distance, rain, crowding, viewing conditions, rest needs, and family coordination created difficulty during the outing.**

# 19. Cross-visit synthesis

## 19.1 Festival is a journey, not a destination

A typical multi-stop experience can be represented as:

**home → decide → travel → park → walk → find destination → queue → darshan/dekhawa → rest → regroup → next stop → return**

The visitor does not simply “visit a Ganpati.” They navigate an evolving sequence.

## 19.2 Festival Pune vs Everyday Pune

A useful conceptual model:

> **Festival Pune is an overlay on Everyday Pune.**

The physical city remains, but the usable mental model changes because of:

- temporary mandals
- crowd flows
- barricades
- parking pressure
- vendor activity
- road control
- installations
- activations
- police management
- medical support
- pedestrian movement

## 19.3 Information exists, but is distributed

Visitors may encounter information through:

- government maps
- signs
- mandal boards
- police/volunteers
- large screens
- public facilities
- live streams
- family/friends
- digital maps
- local knowledge
- observation

The problem is not simply “no information.”

It is:

> **Relevant information is distributed across people, places, temporary infrastructure, digital tools, and moment-to-moment observation.**

## 19.4 Relevance gap

A visitor may still wonder:

- Where should we go next?
- Which stops make sense for our available time?
- What will this place be like?
- How crowded is it right now?
- Where can we rest?
- Where is the toilet?
- Which entrance is easiest?
- Where do we regroup?
- How do we return to parking?
- What am I looking at?
- What makes this Dekhawa meaningful?
- Where can I get help?

Information may exist, but the visitor may still have to **assemble the answer themselves**.

## 19.5 Family as information infrastructure

Examples:

- mother gives meeting directions
- family members help choose a murti remotely
- user tracks grandmother
- friend walks from parking to meet
- older storyteller explains history
- police/volunteers manage movement
- crowds clear a path for ambulance
- sellers make themselves visible

The festival already contains a rich human information network.

The product should support and connect it, not replace it.

# 20. Evidence-backed problem framing

## Working problem space

Visitors navigate a distributed, temporary urban festival while simultaneously deciding:

- where to go
- how long to stay
- how to move
- what to see
- how to accommodate the group
- where to find support
- how to interpret what they experience

Existing information exists in multiple forms and can be contextual rather than centralized.

## Working problem statement

> **Visitors to Pune Ganeshotsav have to piece together route, cultural, practical, and support information across a temporary and constantly changing urban environment, making it harder to decide where to go, understand what they are experiencing, and adapt the journey to the people they are with.**

## Opportunity statement

> **Create a festival companion that connects route planning, cultural discovery, and contextual assistance around the visitor’s actual journey.**

Answers:

# 24. Cultural discovery layer

Core idea:

> **Every Ganpati has a story.**

Discovery should allow:

- Ganpati story
- mandal history
- Dekhawa theme
- cultural significance
- “look for this” details
- optional deeper reading
- future optional audio

Progressive disclosure is important.

Do not create a huge encyclopedia.

Discovery should connect back to the journey:

**Discover → understand → add/navigate → return to journey**

Likewise:

**Destination → story → understand → continue**

---

# 25. Assistance layer

A **Get Help** entry point has been explored for the live journey and the ecosystem.

Possible categories:

- Medical emergency
- Police/safety assistance
- Find family / regroup
- Nearby support/facilities

Critical rule:

Do not claim dispatch, “nearest available ambulance,” hospital availability, or live service status unless verified live data exists.

For a prototype, simulated information must be clearly understood as simulation.

The help action should be accessible but should not dominate the visual experience.

# 26. Home screen — current architecture question

The Home screen is intended as the ecosystem hub.

It should answer:

> **“What do I need to know or do next?”**

Not:

> “What features does the app have?”

### Before a journey

Primary:

**Plan my journey**

Secondary:

**Explore the festival**

Useful support:

- practical information
- facilities
- help

### During a journey

Primary:

**Continue my journey**

Show:

- next stop
- progress
- approximate remaining time
- contextual support
- quick help

This makes the Home screen **stateful** rather than static.

# 30. What has evidence for it

The fieldwork reasonably supports:

1. The festival involves multi-stage movement through urban space.
2. Different destinations can have different crowd conditions.
3. Weather can change walking conditions.
4. Family members can have different mobility/comfort needs.
5. People use phones and other people as navigation/coordination infrastructure.
6. Cultural information already exists through human storytelling and physical Dekhawas.
7. Practical support infrastructure exists.
8. Visitors photograph major installations.
9. Festival infrastructure temporarily changes city movement.
10. Information is encountered from multiple sources.

These support the **problem space** and justify deeper validation.

# 31. What is still hypothesis

Do not present these as proven:

- a large user base would use a dedicated festival app
- users prefer one integrated app to multiple existing tools
- current crowd data can reliably be captured at required granularity
- mandals would supply reliable live information
- facilities information would remain up to date
- visitors would trust route recommendations
- cultural discovery improves satisfaction
- Home is necessarily the ideal primary information architecture
- all visitors need family-centred planning
- the proposed feature set is necessary

These must be validated with additional research and usability testing.

# 33. Evidence-to-design chain

For every feature use:

```text
OBSERVATION
   ↓
PATTERN
   ↓
USER / SYSTEM NEED
   ↓
OPPORTUNITY
   ↓
PRODUCT REQUIREMENT
   ↓
INTERACTION
   ↓
UI
```

### Good example

Observation: user had to keep track of grandmother in a crowded rainy environment.

Pattern: family coordination becomes part of the festival journey.

Need: adapt outing around the person requiring more support.

Opportunity: plan around the people coming with you.

Requirement: mobility/rest needs should alter the route.

Interaction: group selection + needs selection.

UI: generated route changes and explains why.

### Weak example

Feature: Festival Passport.

Reason: “It could make the app engaging.”

This is a design idea, not evidence-backed requirement.

# 36. Design principles

1. **Context over catalogue**
2. **Support the journey, not just the destination**
3. **Plan around people, not only places**
4. **Explain why the system made a recommendation**
5. **Information should be actionable**
6. **Cultural discovery should be optional and contextual**
7. **Assist rather than overwhelm**
8. **Use existing human infrastructure rather than pretending it does not exist**
9. **Be explicit about uncertainty**
10. **Preserve joy and curiosity**
11. **Do not treat the festival as a broken system**
12. **Do not confuse one observation with a universal truth**

# 39. Accessibility principles

Assume use by older adults and visitors with mobility / comfort differences.

Principles:

- readable type
- high contrast
- ≥44 × 44 touch targets
- no essential colour-only status
- explicit words with icons
- clear action-oriented warnings
- layouts that support text expansion
- no tiny metadata carrying critical meaning
- one clear primary action per important screen

# 40. Cultural design rules

Cultural identity should come primarily from:

- real content
- local vocabulary
- authentic destination names
- actual cultural explanations
- real imagery where available
- meaningful information architecture

Avoid cultural decoration that does not communicate anything.

Do not default to:

- Ganpati illustrations
- modak icons everywhere
- lotus motifs
- temple arches
- rangoli patterns
- ornamental borders
- gold foil / gold gradients
- excessive marigold imagery

The interface should feel culturally specific through meaning, not stereotypes or visual ornament.

---

# 41. Language rules

Tone:

- calm
- direct
- practical
- specific
- human
- not marketing copy

Good examples:

> Who are you celebrating Ganeshotsav with?

> We’ll plan the route around them, not just around the mandals.

> What should we account for?

> Rest point in about 3 minutes.

> Currently reported as very crowded.

> Learn the story.

> Get help.

Avoid:

- seamless
- unforgettable
- journey of a lifetime
- AI-powered
- personalized just for you
- excessive exclamation marks

---

# 42. Honesty about uncertainty

For crowd, route time, and facilities use language such as:

- approx.
- usually
- currently reported as
- last updated
- not confirmed today

Do not show:

- fake exact crowd counts
- fake percentages
- fake confidence scores
- “nearest available ambulance” without verification
- guaranteed facility availability

---

# 43. Product exclusions

Avoid introducing:

- onboarding carousel
- login/account setup
- gradients
- glassmorphism
- excessive shadows
- generic hero imagery
- feature-grid dashboard
- charts/statistics/progress rings
- gamification
- social feed
- ratings/reviews
- follower systems
- AI chat assistant
- floating action buttons
- commercial offers
- ticketing/payment
- fake dispatch capabilities
- decorative festival patterns as a substitute for substance

---

# 44. Home screen quality criteria

A successful Home screen should:

- be understandable within seconds
- make the primary next action obvious
- work before leaving home
- work during an active visit
- connect discovery to journey
- surface useful contextual information
- provide accessible help
- avoid looking like a feature dashboard

The screen should answer **what matters now**, not list every capability.

# 47. Success criterion

The strongest success condition is not:

> “The app has many features.”

It is:

> **The visitor can understand what to do next, why the recommendation fits their situation, and where to get relevant context or support without having to assemble the information themselves.**

# 48. Current north star

The desired feeling is:

> **“This understands where I am, who I’m with, what I want to experience, and what I might need next.”**

Not:

> “This app has a lot of features.”

# 50. Case-study argument in plain language

1. Pune Ganeshotsav creates a temporary urban condition.
2. Visitors are simultaneously navigating, choosing, interpreting, coordinating, and adapting.
3. The field visits showed different manifestations of these tasks.
4. Information and support already exist, but through many separate human, physical, and digital sources.
5. The visitor often has to integrate those sources themselves.
6. This creates an opportunity for contextual, journey-centred information assistance.
7. The route prototype addresses one part of the problem.
8. Cultural discovery and assistance complete the experience.
9. Therefore the Home screen can legitimately function as the ecosystem hub — **provided further validation supports this architecture**.

# 53. How to make future decisions

Before introducing any major feature or screen, ask:

1. What observation or evidence led to this?
2. Is it direct observation, documented evidence, interpretation, or hypothesis?
3. What need does it represent?
4. Why does it belong in this ecosystem?
5. What existing human/physical system does it complement?
6. Is the information reliable enough to present?
7. What happens when conditions change?
8. Does it preserve the cultural experience?
9. Can we explain the feature without saying “we thought it would be useful”?

# 54. Final project rule

> **Do not design backwards from finished screens. The UI must be the visible consequence of the research and reasoning.**

The project should always remain defensible as:

**field evidence → system understanding → problem framing → opportunity → requirements → ecosystem → interface**
